What changed on July 1, 2026

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law in July 2025, rewrote federal student loan limits effective for loans first disbursed on or after July 1, 2026. The three biggest changes: the Graduate PLUS Loan program — which previously let grad and professional students borrow up to their full cost of attendance — was eliminated for new borrowers; graduate and professional students now face fixed annual and aggregate caps on Direct Unsubsidized Loans instead; and a new overall lifetime cap of $257,500 applies across a borrower's entire undergraduate, graduate, and professional federal borrowing combined. Parent PLUS loans, which previously had no cap at all, are now limited to $20,000 per year and $65,000 lifetime per undergraduate student for new Parent PLUS borrowers.

Are you actually a "new" borrower?

This is the detail that trips people up most. If you already had a federal Direct Loan disbursed before July 1, 2026 for the exact same degree program at the exact same school, and you've stayed continuously enrolled, you're typically a continuing borrower — meaning you keep access to the prior, generally more generous rules (including Grad PLUS, if you'd used it before) for up to three more years or the rest of your program's length, whichever comes first. Transferring schools, switching programs, or taking a gap in enrollment can reset that status and drop you into the new-borrower rules mid-program.

The "professional degree" list keeps changing

Professional students get a much higher cap than graduate students, so which list your program lands on matters enormously. The Department of Education's official list has already been revised more than once since summer 2026 due to court challenges — several nursing, physical therapy, and psychology programs were removed and then added back. Don't assume your program's status based on how advanced or selective it feels; check the current list directly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the OBBBA and when did the new loan limits start? OBBBA is the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed into law in July 2025. Its federal student loan provisions took effect July 1, 2026, for loans first disbursed on or after that date. Loans you already had before July 1, 2026 are handled under separate "legacy" or "continuing borrower" rules, not the new limits directly.

What counts as a "continuing borrower" (am I grandfathered)? You're generally treated as a continuing borrower if you had at least one Direct Loan (Subsidized, Unsubsidized, or Grad PLUS) disbursed before July 1, 2026 for the same degree program at the same school, and you've stayed continuously enrolled in it. Continuing borrowers can keep borrowing under the prior, generally higher limits — including Grad PLUS if they'd used it before — for up to three additional years or the rest of their program length, whichever is shorter. Changing schools, changing programs, or a gap in enrollment can end continuing-borrower status.

How is "professional" vs "graduate" student decided, and why does it matter? Professional students get a higher cap ($50,000/year, $200,000 aggregate) than graduate students ($20,500/year, $100,000 aggregate). The list of which specific programs count as "professional" has been revised more than once due to ongoing litigation. Check our Professional Degree List tracker or your financial aid office for the current status of your specific program.

Does the $257,500 lifetime cap include Parent PLUS loans? No. The $257,500 aggregate lifetime limit applies to the student's own Direct Loans across their entire undergraduate, graduate, and professional borrowing combined. Parent PLUS loans run on a separate track with their own cap and don't count toward the student's $257,500 figure.

What happens if I hit my new, lower limit and still have a funding gap? The gap has to come from somewhere else: private student loans, payment plans, assistantships or fellowships, scholarships, or savings. Before turning to private loans, check whether your specific graduate or professional program was recently added to or removed from the "professional" list, since that alone can roughly double your federal annual limit.
