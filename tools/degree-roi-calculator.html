How the ROI calculation works

This calculator estimates the financial return on your degree by comparing lifetime earnings with a degree versus without one, then subtracting the total cost including loan interest. A positive ROI means your degree pays for itself financially. A higher ROI means faster payback and greater long-term advantage.

The calculation uses your expected starting salary, an assumed annual growth rate, the salary you'd likely earn without a degree, total degree cost, and your loan total cost (principal + interest).

The starting salary you enter drives most of the result, so use a real figure for your field. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York reports the median annual wage of full-time workers aged 22 to 27 with a bachelor's degree, by major. Across all majors it is $58,000.

School cost matters as much as major

Two students with identical majors and starting salaries can have very different ROIs based on what their degrees cost. A $60,000 total degree cost versus a $200,000 total degree cost produces dramatically different payback timelines — all else equal. The degree market generally does not reward the cost premium of expensive schools outside a narrow set of elite programs where the network effect is real.

The payback period concept

A simpler way to think about ROI: how many years until your earnings premium pays back the total cost of the degree? If your degree costs $100,000 total (including loan interest) and you earn $20,000 more per year than you would without it, the payback period is 5 years. After that, every additional year of work is pure earnings premium.

Most degrees with reasonable total costs and average salary premiums pay back within 10–15 years — well within most working careers.

Non-financial returns

This calculator only measures financial ROI. Degrees also provide professional networks, career flexibility, access to graduate programs, and personal development that have real value but are difficult to quantify. Use this calculator as one input in your decision, not the only one.

How school prestige affects ROI

Outside of a narrow set of elite programs, the job market does not consistently reward the cost premium of expensive schools. A computer science degree from a $15,000/year public university costs about $60,000 over four years. The same degree from a $60,000/year private university costs $240,000. Employers paying $90,000 for a CS graduate do not pay four times more for the private school version — except at a handful of firms that explicitly recruit from top-10 programs. For most careers and most employers, degree ROI heavily favors lower-cost schools.

Opportunity cost: what you are not earning

The full cost of a four-year degree includes not just tuition and fees but the income you forgo by attending full-time instead of working. A student who could have earned $35,000/year working full-time gives up $140,000 in income over four years. This opportunity cost is rarely factored into degree ROI discussions but is real — especially for career-changers or older students who already have work experience. For traditional 18-year-old students with limited work history, the opportunity cost is lower but still nonzero.

When forgiveness changes the math

Public Service Loan Forgiveness forgives the remaining federal balance after 120 qualifying payments while working for a government agency or qualifying nonprofit. For teachers, social workers and public health workers whose ROI looks weak on paper, PSLF can turn it positive. It depends on employer type, loan type and repayment plan, and private loans never qualify, so check your own case with Federal Student Aid's Loan Simulator before building a plan around it.

How to run your own numbers

  • Starting salary: use the figure for your field, ideally your school's own outcomes from the College Scorecard, not a national average.
  • Total cost: net price after grants and scholarships, for every year you expect to attend, plus interest that builds while you are in school.
  • Without-degree salary: what you could realistically earn in the same years without the degree.

If the payback period comes out above about 15 years, treat it as a signal to look at scholarships, a lower-cost school, or a different path to the same career, not necessarily a reason to skip the degree.

Frequently Asked Questions

What counts as a "good" degree ROI? There's no universal cutoff, but a payback period under 10 years is generally considered strong, and under 15–20 years is still reasonable for most careers. A degree that never pays back its total cost over a 40-year career is a red flag worth reconsidering.

Does this calculator account for inflation? No — it uses nominal dollar growth for salary but doesn't separately model inflation eroding purchasing power over decades. Treat the output as a relative comparison (with degree vs without) rather than a precise dollar forecast.

Should I include scholarships and grants in the cost? No — subtract scholarships and grants first, since that money you don't have to earn back. Only include out-of-pocket cost and any loans you'll actually repay.

How accurate is the "Without-Degree Salary" estimate? This varies significantly by field, location, and individual circumstances. Use realistic data for your comparison — median high school graduate earnings in your intended career path, not a national average that may not reflect your specific situation.

Is grad school or law school ROI usually better than a bachelor's degree? It depends heavily on field and school tier. Graduate degrees in high-demand fields (nursing, engineering, computer science) often show strong ROI because the earnings premium is large relative to added cost. Law school ROI varies enormously by school ranking — graduates of top-14 law schools generally see strong payback, while many lower-ranked programs show weak or negative ROI once tuition debt and lost working years are factored in. Run each degree path through this calculator separately using realistic salary data for your target field.

How is degree ROI different from a simple cost-vs-salary comparison? A simple cost-vs-salary comparison ignores what you'd have earned without the degree (opportunity cost) and how long it takes for the extra earnings to actually repay the total investment (payback period). ROI calculators like this one account for both, which is why two degrees with identical starting salaries can have very different real ROI if one costs twice as much or requires two extra years out of the workforce.

Does a degree's ROI improve with scholarships? Yes. Grants and scholarships lower the cost you have to earn back, which shortens the payback period without any change in salary.

Is a low-ROI major automatically a bad choice? No. ROI doesn't capture job satisfaction, mission-driven work, or credentials a career requires. It is one important input, and a reason to keep the cost of the degree low.

How does loan forgiveness affect degree ROI? If you qualify for PSLF or income-driven forgiveness, your effective cost can be much lower than what you borrowed. Use realistic eligibility, not the best case.
