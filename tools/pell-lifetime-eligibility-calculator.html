Remaining Pell Eligibility

The 600% lifetime limit, explained

Since the 2012–13 award year, federal law caps total Pell Grant eligibility at 600% Lifetime Eligibility Used — roughly 12 full-time semesters, or about 6 years, regardless of how many different schools or programs you've attended. Every full-time semester you receive a full scheduled award uses approximately 50% of your total lifetime eligibility; a half-time semester uses roughly 25%. The percentage is tracked cumulatively across your entire academic history dating back to when you first received Pell funding, and it follows you to every school through the National Student Loan Data System (NSLDS).

The reduction between 500% and 600% catches a lot of students off guard, because their financial need hasn't changed — only their remaining lifetime eligibility has. A student at 550% LEU with a $0 SAI doesn't get denied outright; they get a Pell Grant capped at 50% of their scheduled award (600% − 550% = 50% remaining), even though their calculated financial need would otherwise qualify them for the full amount.

Unlike SAP, there's no appeal. If you fail Satisfactory Academic Progress, your school has a formal appeals process for extenuating circumstances. Pell LEU doesn't work that way — it's a hard limit set directly by federal statute, and no institutional appeal or professional judgment override can extend it, no matter the circumstances.

Does it ever reset?

No — and this is the single most common hope students bring to the question. There is no waiting period that clears the counter. Changing colleges does not reset it, transferring credits does not reset it, and stopping out for five or ten years and returning as an adult learner does not reset it. Percentages disbursed a decade ago still count today. The 600% is a lifetime figure in the literal sense.

What does move the number is arithmetic rather than time. Your LEU changes whenever a Pell disbursement is made, and it can also change if your Scheduled Award is adjusted up or down for a year you have already been paid — including, under the current rules, when a school has to return Pell funds because non-federal grants or scholarships equalled or exceeded your cost of attendance.

Three ways eligibility can actually be restored

The Department does restore LEU in specific situations, and because the adjustments are made automatically most students never learn they qualified. All three run through the COD system; your school does not have to file anything, and the corrected figure appears in NSLDS.

The closed-school path is the one worth checking if any part of your history sits at a school that no longer exists. For it to apply you generally need to have been enrolled during the year the school closed or the award year immediately before, and the eligibility is not restored if you completed the credential — the rule exists for students left with nothing to show for the money, not for graduates.

Because these adjustments happen without you asking, the practical move is simply to check the current figure rather than assume the one you remember is still accurate. Your LEU is shown in your account on studentaid.gov, and it is the number that governs — not any estimate, including this one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Pell Grant reset after six years or after a break in school? No. There is no time-based reset. Changing schools, stopping out, transferring credits, or returning years later as an adult learner all leave the counter exactly where it was. The only ways it moves down are the specific restoration and dispute processes below.

Can lifetime eligibility ever be restored? Yes, in three situations: attendance at a school that later closed without you graduating, a qualifying loan discharge on or after 1 July 2017 tied to the same school and award year, or a successful LEU dispute. The Department applies these automatically through its systems, so the corrected figure appears in NSLDS without your school filing anything.

How do I find out exactly how much I have left? Sign in to studentaid.gov and look for your Lifetime Eligibility Used percentage. Subtract it from 600% and divide by 100 to get roughly how many more full-time years of Pell remain. A student showing 75% has used about a semester and a half and has the large majority of their eligibility intact.

What is Pell Grant Lifetime Eligibility Used (LEU)? The cumulative percentage of your total lifetime Pell Grant eligibility you've used, tracked since your first Pell disbursement. The lifetime limit is 600% — about 12 full-time semesters or 6 years. At 600% you can no longer receive Pell funding at all.

How many semesters of Pell Grant do I have left? Subtract your current LEU from 600%, then divide by 50 (one full-time semester typically uses 50% of lifetime eligibility). A student at 300% LEU has 6 full-time semesters left.

Can I appeal my Pell Grant lifetime eligibility limit? No. Unlike SAP, LEU is a hard federal limit with no appeals process or professional judgment override, regardless of when your first Pell funds were received.

What happens when I get close to 600% LEU? You get an email notification at 450%. Between 500% and 600%, your award is capped at your remaining percentage rather than the full scheduled amount. At 600% or higher, no Pell eligibility remains.

Where do I find my current Pell Grant LEU? Log into studentaid.gov with your FSA ID — it's listed in your federal student aid history. Your financial aid office can also look it up via NSLDS.
