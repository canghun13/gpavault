How SAP is actually evaluated: three separate tests

Satisfactory Academic Progress isn't one number — it's three independent standards, and you must pass all three to keep your federal aid. This calculator checks each one against your numbers and, if your completion rate is the problem, shows exactly how many additional credits you'd need to pass — with zero more Ws or Fs — to climb back above the threshold.

Why 67% specifically — it's not an arbitrary number

The 67% pace threshold isn't picked out of thin air. It's the exact mathematical floor implied by the 150% maximum timeframe rule: if your completion rate never drops below two-thirds, then by the time you've attempted 150% of your program's required credits, you will have earned at least 100% of them — precisely enough to finish. Drop below 67% pace for very long, and the arithmetic no longer allows you to finish before hitting the 150% cap, which is exactly why schools flag it early rather than waiting until the timeframe is nearly exhausted.

What counts against your completion rate (and what doesn't)

Every course you're still enrolled in past the add/drop deadline counts as attempted, regardless of the grade you end up with. Only courses you pass count as earned. That means withdrawals (W), failing grades (F), and incompletes (I) all drag your pace down even though a plain W has zero effect on your GPA — the two standards measure completely different things. Repeated courses count as attempted every time, but typically only the passing attempt counts toward earned credits, so retaking a class helps your GPA and eventually your pace, but the failed attempt stays on the attempted side of the ledger permanently.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Satisfactory Academic Progress (SAP)? SAP is a federally required review, run by your financial aid office, that checks whether you're on track to graduate — separately from any academic standing decision made by the registrar. It has three parts: a minimum cumulative GPA (usually 2.0), a completion rate of at least 67%, and a maximum timeframe of 150% of your program length. You must pass all three to keep receiving federal aid.

Why is the completion rate threshold exactly 67%? It's the mathematical floor implied by the 150% maximum timeframe rule — maintain at least a two-thirds pace and you'll have earned 100% of your required credits by the time you've attempted 150% of them, which is exactly enough to finish.

Does academic probation mean the same thing as failing SAP? No. Academic probation is an enrollment decision made by the registrar based on your school's own GPA policy. SAP is a separate, federally required financial-aid review. You can be on probation while still meeting SAP, or vice versa — check both independently. See our Probation vs. Suspension vs. Dismissal guide for the academic-standing side.

Do W (withdrawal) and F grades count against my completion rate? Yes — both count as credits attempted but not earned, which lowers your pace even though a W alone doesn't touch your GPA. See our guide on how withdrawing affects financial aid for the full breakdown.

What happens if I don't meet SAP? Most schools place you on a financial aid warning for one term first, during which you keep your aid while working to meet the standards. Still short after that? You typically move to suspension and lose aid unless you file a successful appeal — see our SAP appeal guide for what schools actually approve.
