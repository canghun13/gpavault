What "last 60 credits" means

Instead of averaging every course you have ever taken, the school starts with your most recent term and counts backward until it reaches 60 semester credits — roughly your junior and senior years. Quarter-system transcripts use 90 quarter credits for the same span. The idea is to judge you on the work closest to graduate study, so a rough first year matters less.

You calculate it the same way as any GPA: add up the quality points (credits × grade points) for the courses in the window and divide by the credits in the window. Entering each term's graded credits and term GPA, as the calculator does, gives the same result because credits × term GPA equals that term's quality points.

Two details change the number more than people expect. First, the term that crosses the 60-credit line: Middle Tennessee State counts that whole term, while other schools may stop at exactly 60, which is what the "count only credits needed" option estimates. Second, what's inside the window: Middle Tennessee State counts every college-level course with a grade, including repeated courses and transfer courses that didn't transfer because of a low grade. UW–Madison tells applicants not to convert grades to a 4.00 scale at all; it uses your institution's own scale.

Example: a 2.95 cumulative that clears a 3.00 cutoff

A student with 117 graded credits has a 2.95 cumulative GPA after a slow start (2.2, 2.3 and 2.5 in the first three terms) and a strong finish (3.2, 3.4, 3.6, 3.8). Their newest four terms add up to 57 credits, so the 15-credit spring term before them crosses the line.

Raising a last-60 GPA

Because the window rolls forward, every new term you add pushes your oldest credits out. That makes the last-60 GPA far easier to move than a cumulative GPA: a strong term replaces a weak one instead of being averaged against everything. Post-baccalaureate courses taken after your degree generally count as undergraduate-level work in this window if they are undergraduate courses, which is why some applicants take a term of extra coursework before applying. A later master's-level course is not the same thing, and schools treat graduate coursework differently — the University of Arizona, for example, mixes it in only when an applicant has fewer than 12 graduate units. Check with the program before enrolling for that reason.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my last 60 credits GPA? Start with your most recent term and count backward until you reach 60 semester credits (90 quarter credits). Multiply each course's credits by its grade points, add those quality points, and divide by the credits in the window. If a term crosses the 60-credit line, your school decides whether to count the whole term or only part of it.

Do repeated courses count in the last 60 credits? At many schools, yes. Middle Tennessee State, for example, counts every college-level course with a grade, including repeats and transfer courses. Ask the program you're applying to, because some follow your registrar's replacement policy instead.

Do post-bacc classes count toward my last 60 GPA? Undergraduate courses you take after your degree generally fall inside the window because they're your most recent coursework. Graduate courses are handled differently by each school — the University of Arizona combines them only when you have fewer than 12 graduate units.

What if my school uses quarters? Use 90 quarter credits instead of 60 semester credits. Arizona State and the Cal State system both state the window as 60 semester or 90 quarter units.

Is the last 60 credits the same as junior and senior year GPA? Usually close, since 60 credits is about two full-time years. It isn't identical if you took summer classes, a light or heavy load, or extra coursework after graduating, so calculate the credits rather than assuming two years.

What GPA do grad schools want in the last 60 credits? Common minimums are 3.00, used by UW–Madison's Graduate School and Middle Tennessee State, and 2.5 at Cal State campuses. Individual programs often set higher bars.
