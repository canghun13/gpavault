Does Dual Enrollment Affect Your College GPA?

8 min read · Updated October 2026

Dual enrollment is pitched to families as a straightforward bargain: college credit at high school prices, a head start on the degree, sometimes a semester shaved off the bill. Most of that is true.

What tends not to come up at the information session is that you are enrolling as a college student. The college that teaches the course opens a permanent academic record in your name, and your grade goes on it. That record does not close when you graduate high school. It follows you into graduate and professional school applications a decade later, and until very recently it followed you into law school applications too.

Whether that matters depends entirely on who is doing the calculating — and one of the answers is about to change.

You end up with more than one GPA

The confusion here is real, and it comes from the fact that "your GPA" is not one number.

  • Your high school GPA includes the dual enrollment grade, usually weighted, and it feeds class rank.
  • Your university GPA usually does not. Most degree-granting institutions accept the credit hours but not the grade points, so the course satisfies a requirement while your institutional GPA starts fresh at zero credits.
  • Your admissions GPA — the one an application service calculates from every transcript you have ever generated — is where it counts, and where the rules differ by profession.

The distinction that matters: AP and IB credit is awarded on an exam score. There is no course grade on a college transcript, so there is nothing for a GPA calculation to find. Dual enrollment produces an actual grade at an actual institution — which is exactly what makes it valuable as credit and durable as a record.

Medical schools count all of it

AMCAS includes grades and credit hours from college-level courses taken while in high school, and it does so even when those credits were never transferred to your undergraduate school or applied toward your degree. The courses appear on the application with a year designation of High School, but they are folded into the cumulative GPA and, where applicable, the science GPA that every school receives.

Two practical consequences follow. You have to request a transcript from every college that holds a record for you — including one where you took a single course at sixteen and never went back. And retaking the course later does not repair the number, because application services generally include every attempt, so the retake averages with the original instead of replacing it.

That last point catches people badly. Your own university may forgive a grade on its institutional transcript, and that forgiveness is genuinely useful for scholarship thresholds and graduation requirements. It has no effect on what AMCAS reports.

Law schools are changing the rule — from the 2027–2028 cycle

LSAC announced in July 2026 that the LSAC GPA would no longer include grades from college-level courses completed while a candidate was in high school, originally effective 28 July 2026. On 18 August 2026 LSAC delayed the change to summer 2027: it now applies to the 2027–2028 applicant cycle onward, and for the 2026–2027 cycle dual enrollment grades still count in the LSAC GPA. Law schools will still receive your full transcripts in the CAS report, and LSAC says applicants who want to highlight strong dual enrollment work can add an addendum.

LSAC's stated reasoning is unusually candid. Access to college courses varies widely between high schools, and where access does exist, students frequently are not told — or are advised incorrectly — whether the grades can affect their law school chances. The exclusion is meant to let high school students take college courses without silently mortgaging an application they have not begun to think about.

For anyone applying in 2027–2028 or later, this is a genuine reprieve. Threads about a ruined LSAC GPA traced back to a course taken at fifteen or sixteen have been a recurring feature for years.

The boundary people get wrong

The exclusion is defined by when you took the course, not what the program was called.

Once the change takes effect, a course completed while you were in high school comes out of the LSAC calculation. A course taken at a community college while you were already enrolled at a university stays in, even if your school labelled it dual enrollment, and even if you took it for exactly the same reasons. It is ordinary undergraduate coursework, and it counts like any other.

So a student with a weak grade from ninth grade and another weak grade from a summer community college course during sophomore year of college will have two grades treated in opposite ways by the same calculation.

So should you do it?

For most students, yes. Dual enrollment saves real money, shortens time to degree, and is a genuine advantage for students whose high schools offer little else. From the 2027–2028 cycle, the LSAC change removes the GPA downside for anyone heading toward law (the grades still appear on the transcripts schools see).

The honest framing is narrower than "avoid it." Treat a dual enrollment course as what it literally is — a college course, taken for a college grade, recorded on a college transcript — rather than as a high school class with a bonus attached. If you are confident of a strong result, take it and take the credit. If you are on the fence about a difficult subject at fifteen, AP caps your downside at not earning the credit, which is a much cheaper failure mode. And if you are already carrying grades you regret, find out now which application services will see them, because the answer is no longer the same for everyone.

Frequently asked questions

Does dual enrollment show up on a college transcript? Yes, permanently, at the college that taught the course — whether or not the credit ever transferred anywhere.

Will dual enrollment grades lower my university GPA? Usually not. Most universities take the credit hours but not the grade points, so your institutional GPA starts fresh.

Do medical schools count dual enrollment in the GPA? Yes — AMCAS includes it even when the credit never transferred, listed under a High School year designation.

Did law schools stop counting dual enrollment grades? Not yet. LSAC delayed the change: dual enrollment grades still count for the 2026–2027 cycle and are excluded starting with the 2027–2028 cycle.

Does the LSAC exclusion cover a community college course I took during college? No. It turns on when you took the course, not the program label.

Can I retake a dual enrollment course to fix the grade? Not for admissions. Services generally average every attempt; institutional grade forgiveness does not travel.

Is AP safer than dual enrollment? Lower downside, yes — a weak AP result just means no credit, with nothing on a college transcript.

Before you rely on this

Policies here change, as the LSAC change and its August 2026 delay demonstrate, and each application service maintains its own conventions for scales, withdrawals, and pass/fail grades. Confirm the current rules directly with the service you will apply through and with the registrar at every college holding a record for you.
