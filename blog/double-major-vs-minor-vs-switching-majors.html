The trade-off most comparisons skip sits in every column: each path costs time, and time has a financial aid consequence that is not visible until late in your degree.

The financial aid limit a double major can quietly hit

Federal aid carries a Satisfactory Academic Progress rule called the maximum timeframe: you can receive aid only while you have attempted no more than 150% of the credits your degree program requires. For a 120-credit bachelor's degree, that ceiling is 180 attempted credits.

The part students do not expect is that adding a second major does not raise that ceiling. Financial aid offices at UCLA, UC Riverside, Colorado State, Buffalo State, and elsewhere state it plainly: a double major or minor gives you no additional eligibility beyond the maximum timeframe for your degree. The 180 credits cover your first major, your second major, your minor, your general education, and everything else together.

Every credit you have ever attempted counts toward it — transfer credit, AP credit your school accepted, courses you withdrew from, courses you failed, courses you retook, and credits from a major you switched out of. That is why the double major tends to become a problem for students who have also changed majors or transferred, rather than for students who planned it from day one.

You can lose aid before you actually reach 180

Aid does not stop only when you cross the line. Federal rules also end eligibility the moment it becomes mathematically impossible to finish within the limit — attempted credits plus credits still required.

That last student has not attempted 180 credits yet, but because 150 plus 36 exceeds the ceiling, aid can end immediately rather than at the point they cross it. Some schools send a warning well before this — Colorado State, for instance, flags students at 130% — but many do not, and the first notice can be a suspended aid package.

Double major versus dual degree is not a technicality

These sound interchangeable and are treated very differently. A double major is one degree with two majors attached, so it keeps the single-degree ceiling. A dual degree is two separate degrees, which typically requires more total credits — and because the maximum timeframe is calculated from the program's published length, a larger program carries a larger ceiling. Buffalo State gives the example of a 150-credit dual degree with a 225-credit maximum timeframe.

For a student with many credits in two genuinely different fields who is running out of room, formally switching into a dual degree program can restore eligibility where a double major cannot. Not every institution offers this route and not every pair of fields qualifies, so it is a conversation for your academic advisor and financial aid office together, before you hit the limit rather than after.

The simplest escape runs the other way. Dropping the second major reduces the credits still required, which can pull you back under the ceiling immediately. If it comes to a choice between the second major and the aid that pays for your final semesters, that is usually the arithmetic that decides it.

Two other clocks that run at the same time

Pell Grant lifetime eligibility is capped at 600%, roughly six years of full-time Pell. An extra fifth year spent finishing a second major uses a full 100% of that cap, which matters if graduate study or a later career change is in the picture.

Federal loan aggregate limits for undergraduates are $31,000 for dependent students and $57,500 for independent ones. A fifth year of borrowing can reach those caps before the degree is finished, and many institutional scholarships stop after eight semesters regardless. A second major is often worth it — but the fifth year it can require is paid for from limits that do not reset.

The question that actually decides it

Every generic guide asks "what are your goals," but the more useful question is narrower: do you still want your original major, or not? If yes, you're choosing between a double major and a minor — both keep your original path intact, so the decision comes down to how much depth you want in the second field versus how much extra time and cost you're willing to take on. If no, none of that matters — a minor in your new interest doesn't solve the problem of a major you no longer want, and switching is the more honest answer even though it usually carries the largest cost.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the pros and cons of a double major versus a minor? A double major gives full depth in two fields but takes the most credits and carries the highest risk of an extra semester. A minor adds a complementary skill in 15 to 24 credits and usually fits inside four years, but carries less weight with employers.

Does a double major extend my financial aid eligibility? No. The 150% maximum timeframe is based on your degree program, so a 120-credit degree allows 180 attempted credits whether you have one major or two. A second major or a minor gives no additional aid eligibility.

What is the difference between a double major and a dual degree for financial aid? A double major is one degree with two majors and keeps the single-degree limit. A dual degree is two separate degrees, usually requiring more credits, and because the limit is based on program length it typically carries a larger maximum timeframe.

Does a double major cost more than a major and a minor? Usually yes, since a double major requires completing full major-level coursework (typically 30-40+ credits) rather than the roughly 15-24 credits a minor requires. A double major is far less likely to fully overlap with your existing requirements.

Do employers actually care about a double major? It depends on the employer and role. For some technical or specialized positions a second major signals real depth, while for many other roles what you can demonstrably do matters far more than the credential on paper.

How do I know if switching majors entirely makes more sense than adding a minor? If you no longer want your original major at all, adding a minor doesn't solve that. Switching makes more sense when your interest in the original field has genuinely faded, not just when you've discovered a second interest alongside it.

Can I decide later, after starting one path? Often yes, especially early in college. Talk to your academic advisor about how late you can make this kind of change without losing progress.
