Average Starting Salary by Major (2026): Is Your Degree Paying Off?

10 min read · Updated October 2026

Choosing a major is one of the biggest financial decisions you will make, and most students choose it without knowing what graduates in their field earn. This guide uses the Federal Reserve Bank of New York's figures for recent graduates to show what each major pays early in a career, explains what drives the gaps, and shows how to check those numbers against your student loans.

How to read these numbers: the New York Fed reports the median annual wage of full-time workers aged 22 to 27 who hold a bachelor's degree, by major, using Census Bureau American Community Survey data. Across all majors the figure is $58,000. These are actual earnings a few years out, not offer letters, so they run lower than employer surveys of starting offers. For comparison, NACE's Winter 2026 Salary Survey projects average starting offers of $81,535 for computer science and $66,155 for social sciences graduates.

STEM majors

Engineering and computing lead every ranking of early-career pay. Computer engineering and computer science graduates typically start as software developers or systems engineers; mechanical, electrical and civil engineers move into design and project roles. Biology is the exception within STEM.

The debt-to-salary rule

The most useful benchmark for college affordability: your total student loan debt at graduation should not exceed your expected first-year salary. If you plan to earn $55,000, try to keep total debt under $55,000. Go $120,000 into debt for a $40,000/year job, and you will spend most of your twenties and some of your thirties paying it down — with limited resources for anything else.

This rule is not absolute. Teachers with loan debt often qualify for PSLF forgiveness after 10 years, which changes the math entirely. High-demand fields like nursing allow accelerated income growth through overtime and specialty certification. Run your specific numbers rather than relying on the rule alone.

What these numbers don't tell you

Starting salary is a snapshot. Career trajectory matters over a 40-year working life. Geography is a major variable — $55,000 in rural Ohio is comfortable; the same salary in San Francisco barely covers rent. Entry-level experience, internships, and certifications can move starting salary meaningfully within any field.

The most important thing these numbers tell you is where the floor is. They set expectations so that your borrowing decision is informed, not hopeful. A student borrowing $100,000 for a field with a $40,000 starting salary deserves to know that number before they sign.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average starting salary for a college graduate? The Federal Reserve Bank of New York puts the median annual wage for full-time bachelor's degree holders aged 22 to 27 at $58,000 across all majors. Employer surveys of starting offers run higher: NACE's Winter 2026 survey projects $81,535 for computer science and $66,155 for social sciences.

Which majors pay the most right out of college? In the New York Fed data, computer engineering ($90,000), computer science ($87,000), and aerospace and chemical engineering ($85,000 each) have the highest early-career median wages.

Which majors pay the least early in a career? Pharmacy at the bachelor's level ($40,000), theology ($41,600), social services ($43,000), and performing arts ($44,000) are at the bottom, followed by a group of education, arts and liberal arts majors at about $45,000.

Does location affect starting salary? Yes. The same job can pay much more in a high-cost metro area than in a lower-cost region, though housing costs often narrow the difference in what the pay actually buys.

Do these figures include signing bonuses? No. The New York Fed figures are annual wages reported in the Census Bureau's American Community Survey. Signing bonuses and stock awards, common in tech and finance, are not captured as separate items.

Is starting salary the best measure of a major's value? No. It is a snapshot of the first few years. Earnings growth, job security, and whether a graduate degree is needed all matter, and so does how much you borrowed to get the degree.
