Student Loan Repayment Plans Explained: Standard, IBR, RAP (2026)

15 min read · Updated October 2026

The federal student loan repayment landscape changed significantly in 2026. The SAVE plan ended through a court-approved settlement: on March 9, 2026 the Eighth Circuit ordered the settlement between the Education Department and the challenging states entered as final judgment, and the district court did so on March 10. About 7 million borrowers were left in interest-free forbearance, and those months do not count toward forgiveness or PSLF. The Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) opened July 1, 2026. SAVE borrowers are being moved out in waves: each gets a 90-day notice from their servicer, the Department said no one had to leave SAVE before September 29, 2026, and anyone who does not pick a plan by their deadline is placed in the Standard Plan, which may cost far more per month than an income-driven plan. Here is every plan explained, with exact payment examples, and how to decide which one fits your situation.

Income-driven plans (IBR, PAYE, RAP) forgive any balance left at the end of the term; Standard, Graduated, Extended and Tiered Standard pay the loan off. "Disc. income" is discretionary income, your AGI minus 150% of the poverty guideline. The plans on the list depend on when you borrowed. If you take out any new federal loan on or after July 1, 2026, your choices for all your Direct Loans narrow to two: RAP or the new Tiered Standard Plan, whose term depends on your balance (10 years under $25,000; 15 years for $25,000–$49,999; 20 years for $50,000–$99,999; 25 years at $100,000 or more). If all your loans predate July 2026, you keep access to IBR, and PAYE and ICR remain open until July 1, 2028, when borrowers still on them must move.

Standard Plan: the financially optimal choice if you can afford it

The Standard Plan divides your balance into 120 equal monthly payments over 10 years. No income verification, no annual recertification, no complexity. You pay the same amount every month for 10 years and the loan is gone.

It minimizes total interest paid because you eliminate principal faster. Here is the cost comparison at the 2026–27 undergraduate rate of 6.52%, computed with the standard amortization formula:

One important note about PSLF: 10-year Standard payments count as qualifying payments, but after 120 of them the loan is already paid off, so there is nothing left to forgive. PSLF only saves money when you are on an income-driven plan (IBR or RAP) whose payment is lower than the Standard amount. If you are aiming for PSLF, pick the income-driven plan and do not pay extra.

Graduated Plan: lower start, higher finish

Graduated payments start lower and increase every two years, completing repayment in 10 years. Designed for borrowers who expect their income to grow significantly in the early years. Total interest paid is higher than the Standard Plan because early payments are smaller and more interest accumulates, but the graduated structure can ease cash flow pressure in the first few years post-graduation.

Graduated payments do not count toward PSLF, so it is only for borrowers who are certain their income will increase substantially and who are not pursuing PSLF. If your income growth is uncertain, the Standard Plan's predictability is easier to budget around.

IBR: the core safety net plan

IBR caps your monthly payment based on your income and family size. For new borrowers (after July 1, 2014), the cap is 10% of discretionary income. For older loans, it is 15%. Discretionary income is roughly your adjusted gross income minus 150% of the federal poverty guideline for your family size.

What this means in practice: if your income is low enough, your IBR payment can be very low — potentially zero. Your loan does not default just because you pay $0; you remain in good standing. Interest continues to accrue, but IBR's interest protections prevent your balance from growing in many cases.

The trade-off: the loan term extends to 20 or 25 years, and any remaining balance at the end is forgiven. The temporary federal tax exclusion for income-driven forgiveness expired after December 31, 2025, so forgiveness at the end of IBR, PAYE or RAP is generally taxable federal income again (PSLF forgiveness stays tax-free). For borrowers with high debt-to-income ratios, IBR often provides necessary financial breathing room.

IBR is the most flexible income-driven option for most borrowers and the most common plan for PSLF candidates. You must recertify your income annually — missing recertification is the most common IBR mistake and can cause your payment to spike suddenly.

PAYE (Pay As You Earn)

PAYE caps payments at 10% of discretionary income (same as new-borrower IBR) with a 20-year forgiveness timeline. To qualify, you must be a new borrower as of October 1, 2007 with a Direct Loan disbursed after October 1, 2011, and your payment under PAYE must be lower than your Standard Plan payment.

For borrowers who first borrowed after July 1, 2014, PAYE and IBR work almost the same way: 10% of discretionary income, 20 years to forgiveness, payment capped at the 10-year Standard amount. PAYE's edge is for borrowers with older loans, who are stuck with the 15% / 25-year version of IBR. PAYE is scheduled to close on July 1, 2028, so anyone on it today will have to move to IBR or RAP by then.

RAP: the new plan replacing SAVE (July 1, 2026)

The Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) replaces SAVE as of July 1, 2026. Here are the key features and what they mean practically:

  • Payment structure: A percentage of your full adjusted gross income (AGI) that rises one point per $10,000: AGI of $10,001–$20,000 pays 1%, $20,001–$30,000 pays 2%, and so on up to 10% above $100,000. The annual amount is divided by 12, then $50 a month is subtracted for each dependent. Unlike IBR and PAYE, which use discretionary income, RAP uses total AGI.
  • Minimum payment: $10 per month, including for AGI of $10,000 or less. RAP has no $0 payment, which IBR and PAYE allow for very low incomes.
  • Interest and principal protection: Interest your payment does not cover is waived, not added to your balance. If your payment reduces principal by less than $50 in a month, the government cuts your principal by up to $50 more. On RAP, a balance cannot grow and usually shrinks every month.
  • Forgiveness timeline: 360 qualifying payments (30 years), longer than the 20 years on new-borrower IBR and PAYE. Qualifying payments you made under IBR, PAYE, ICR or SAVE before switching generally carry over toward the 360.
  • PSLF compatibility: RAP payments count toward Public Service Loan Forgiveness, which still forgives at 120 payments. Parent PLUS loans cannot use RAP.

For borrowers who previously had very low or $0 SAVE payments, RAP's $10 minimum is a concrete change. For most borrowers with moderate incomes, the practical effect depends on whether the AGI-based calculation produces a higher or lower payment than their old discretionary income calculation. Use the Federal Student Aid loan simulator at studentaid.gov before switching plans.

PSLF: the most valuable program most people underuse

Public Service Loan Forgiveness forgives your entire remaining federal Direct Loan balance after 120 qualifying payments (10 years) while working full-time for a government or qualifying nonprofit employer. The forgiven balance is currently tax-free, and there is no dollar cap on forgiveness.

To qualify, every element must be in place simultaneously:

  • Direct Loans only — FFEL and Perkins loans do not qualify without consolidation first
  • On an income-driven repayment plan or Standard Plan
  • Full-time employment at a qualifying employer (government agency, 501(c)(3) nonprofit)
  • 120 qualifying payments — these do not need to be consecutive, but each one must meet all criteria

Submit the PSLF Form annually, not just at year 10. Annual submission identifies problems while you still have time to fix them. Borrowers who wait until year 10 to submit sometimes discover years of payments did not qualify — a preventable and costly mistake.

For teachers, nurses, social workers, government employees, and nonprofit workers with significant loan balances, PSLF can mean six-figure forgiveness. It fundamentally changes the repayment math and should inform your plan choice from the first payment.

How to choose the right plan

The decision comes down to three factors: your income relative to your debt, your employer type, and your timeline.

  • If you can afford standard payments comfortably: Standard Plan is almost always the best financial choice. You pay the least total interest and the loan disappears in 10 years.
  • If your payment-to-income ratio is high: An income-driven plan (IBR or RAP) reduces monthly burden and keeps you in good standing while your income grows. A payment above 10% of gross monthly income is generally the threshold where IDR makes sense.
  • If you work in public service or nonprofit: IBR or RAP plus PSLF is often the optimal strategy — even if you could afford standard payments. Every dollar of remaining balance forgiven after 120 payments is tax-free. Paying extra beyond the minimum reduces your forgiveness benefit.
  • If you have older loans (before July 2014) and no PSLF path: PAYE's 10% / 20-year terms beat old IBR's 15% / 25 years, but PAYE closes July 1, 2028. Compare it with RAP, which is often cheaper per month but runs 30 years.
  • If you were on SAVE and haven't switched yet: Act now — time spent in SAVE forbearance does not count toward forgiveness, so every month you wait is forgiveness progress permanently lost. Compare IBR and RAP using the Loan Simulator, then enroll at studentaid.gov rather than waiting for your 90-day notice, because the automatic fallback is the Standard Plan. The AGI vs discretionary income calculation can go either way depending on your specific income and family size.

Common repayment mistakes to avoid

  • Missing annual IDR recertification: If you miss the recertification deadline, your payment can spike to the standard amount instantly. Set a calendar reminder at 10 months.
  • Paying extra on an IDR plan with PSLF: If you are pursuing PSLF, every extra dollar paid reduces your eventual forgiveness by the same amount. There is no financial benefit to prepaying under PSLF.
  • Not consolidating FFEL loans: FFEL loans do not qualify for PSLF or most IDR plans without first consolidating into a Direct Consolidation Loan. If you have older loans, check your loan type on studentaid.gov.
  • Choosing extended repayment to lower payments: Extended plans lower your monthly payment but do not qualify for income-driven forgiveness or PSLF. IBR or RAP almost always makes more sense than extended repayment for borrowers who need payment relief.

Frequently asked questions

Is RAP better than the old IBR? It depends on your income and household. At $50,000 AGI a single borrower pays $166.67 a month on RAP versus $217.17 on new-borrower IBR, but at $75,000 RAP is higher ($437.50 vs $425.50), and at very low incomes IBR can be $0 while RAP is at least $10. RAP's forgiveness takes 30 years versus 20 on new-borrower IBR. Run both scenarios through the studentaid.gov Loan Simulator before choosing.

Is PAYE still available in 2026? Only for existing borrowers who took out all their loans before July 1, 2026 and don't take out new federal loans afterward — and only until PAYE is phased out on July 1, 2028. New borrowers after July 1, 2026 can't enroll in PAYE and are limited to RAP or the Standard Plan.

What happened to the SAVE plan? SAVE ended through a court-approved settlement: the Eighth Circuit ordered it entered as final judgment on March 9, 2026, and the district court signed it on March 10. SAVE borrowers sit in interest-free forbearance that does not count toward forgiveness or PSLF. Servicers are sending 90-day notices in waves (no one had to leave before September 29, 2026); if you do not choose IBR, RAP or another plan by your deadline, you are placed in the Standard Plan. You can switch now at studentaid.gov without waiting for the notice.

Does RAP have a payment cap like IBR does? No — IBR caps your monthly payment at what you'd pay under the 10-year Standard Repayment Plan, no matter how high your income climbs. RAP has no equivalent ceiling; it's a straight percentage of your AGI, so a higher-income borrower with a relatively small remaining balance can end up paying more under RAP than under IBR, or even Standard repayment itself.

If I switch from RAP to IBR, do my RAP payments still count toward forgiveness? Not for IBR's forgiveness clock — this is a one-way street. Switching from IBR into RAP carries your prior payment count over, but switching out of RAP into IBR (or another IDR plan) does not carry RAP payments forward. RAP payments do still count toward PSLF's 120-payment count regardless of later plan switches.
