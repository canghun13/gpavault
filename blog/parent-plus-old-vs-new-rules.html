Parent PLUS Loans: Old Rules vs. New OBBBA Rules

9 min read · Updated September 2026
Advertisement · Google AdSense

Parent PLUS loans changed more than almost any other federal loan program on July 1, 2026 — and the change that's easiest to miss isn't the borrowing cap everyone's talking about. It's that Parent PLUS lost its only income-driven repayment option entirely for new loans. Here's the full before-and-after.

The ICR loss is the part families are missing

News coverage of OBBBA has focused heavily on the new borrowing caps, but Income-Contingent Repayment was the only income-driven option Parent PLUS borrowers ever had — regular federal student loans have several IDR-style options, but Parent PLUS only ever had ICR. Losing it for new loans means any Parent PLUS loan taken out from now on is locked into the Standard repayment plan, with fixed payments regardless of the family's income. The one way to preserve ICR eligibility was consolidating an existing Parent PLUS loan into a Direct Consolidation Loan before July 1, 2026 — a step that required weeks of processing time, meaning the realistic deadline to start had already passed by the time many families learned about it.

Is the $65,000 limit per parent or per student?

Per student. The $20,000 annual and $65,000 aggregate limits attach to the dependent student, not to the parent who signs the loan. Two things follow, and they cut in opposite directions.

First, both parents share one limit. If a mother and father each take out a Parent PLUS loan for the same child, their combined borrowing for that child cannot exceed $20,000 in a year or $65,000 in total. A second parent applying does not open a second $65,000.

Second, each child gets a separate limit. There is no per-parent ceiling across siblings, so a parent borrowing for two children in college has up to $65,000 available for each of them.

Parent PLUS also sits outside the student's own limits. It does not use up any of the student's $31,000 or $57,500 undergraduate aggregate, and it is excluded from the new $257,500 lifetime cap on the student's federal loans. The loan limit calculator handles the student's side separately.

The fourth-year squeeze

The two caps do not fit together evenly over a four-year degree. Borrowing the full $20,000 every year would reach $80,000, but the aggregate stops at $65,000, so the last year is where the gap appears:

A family that relied on the full $20,000 in each of the first three years has $15,000 less available in year four than it did before, with the same bill. A fifth year gets nothing from Parent PLUS at all. If you expect to borrow near the annual maximum, it is usually better to plan the four-year total up front, for example about $16,250 a year, than to find the shortfall in the final year when there is the least time to arrange another source.

Who keeps the old rules, and the younger-sibling trap

Families already borrowing can stay on the old, uncapped structure for a while. The interim exception, as schools are describing it, applies when the student was enrolled in the program as of June 30, 2026 and a Direct Loan for that program was disbursed before July 1, 2026. It lasts for the lesser of three academic years or the time left in the program, and only while the student stays continuously enrolled in the same program at the same school.

  • Started as a freshman in fall 2025: one year completed, three left in a four-year program. The window covers 2026–27 through 2028–29, which is on-time graduation. A fifth year would fall under the new limits.
  • Started in fall 2024: two years left, so the window covers 2026–27 and 2027–28.
  • Transfers, changes program, or takes a gap: the window can end early and the new limits apply from that point.
  • Starts college in fall 2026 or later: no loan was disbursed for that program before July 1, 2026, so the new $20,000 and $65,000 limits apply from the first year.

That last line is the one that surprises families with more than one child. Because the exception is tied to the student's program, being on the old rules for an older child does not appear to carry over to a younger sibling who starts college after July 1, 2026. The parent can be uncapped for one child and capped at $20,000 a year for the other at the same time.

One point the published guidance does not settle is whether Parent PLUS you borrowed under the old rules counts toward the $65,000 once the window ends, for example in a fifth year. Schools' own summaries leave it open. If that year matters to your plan, ask the financial aid office directly and get the answer in writing.

What to do if you need to borrow now

  • Check the student's own federal borrowing room first. Federal student loans (not Parent PLUS) generally carry more borrower protections. Use the OBBBA Loan Limit Calculator to see exactly how much is available.
  • Model the new $20,000/year cap against your actual need — for many families at higher-cost schools, this alone leaves a meaningful funding gap that needs another source. The college cost calculator gives you the four-year total to plan against.
  • Plan the $65,000 across all four years instead of taking $20,000 each year and running short in year four.
  • Compare private loan options carefully if the gap remains, since private loans also lack income-driven repayment but may offer more competitive rates for well-qualified borrowers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the new $20,000/$65,000 Parent PLUS cap apply to loans I already have? No. The new caps apply only to new Parent PLUS borrowers taking out their first loan for a given student on or after July 1, 2026. Continuing borrowers can keep the prior uncapped structure for a transition period.

What happened to Income-Contingent Repayment (ICR) for Parent PLUS loans? ICR was the only income-driven option ever available to Parent PLUS borrowers, and any new loan disbursed on or after July 1, 2026 is no longer eligible. Existing loans could preserve ICR only by consolidating before that date.

If I missed the consolidation deadline, do I have any income-driven options left? Options are limited — new Parent PLUS loans are restricted to the Standard repayment plan. Talk to your loan servicer about deferment or forbearance if payments become unaffordable.

Should I take out a Parent PLUS loan or have my child borrow more instead? This now requires more comparison than before. Check the student's own remaining federal borrowing room first, since federal student loans generally carry more protections than Parent PLUS.

Is the $65,000 Parent PLUS limit per parent or per student? Per dependent student. If both parents borrow for the same child, their combined Parent PLUS for that child is capped at $20,000 a year and $65,000 in total. A second parent borrowing does not add a second limit.

If I have two children in college, do I get $65,000 for each? Yes. The limit is per dependent student, so each child has a separate $65,000. There is no overall per-parent cap across siblings.

Do Parent PLUS loans count against my child's own federal loan limits? No. Parent PLUS is the parent's debt. It does not use up the student's undergraduate aggregate limit, and it is excluded from the $257,500 lifetime cap on the student's federal loans.
