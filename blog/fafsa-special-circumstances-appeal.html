FAFSA Special Circumstances Appeal: How to Request a Professional Judgment

8 min read · Updated October 2026

Your FAFSA is based on tax information from up to two years earlier, which means it can badly misrepresent your family's actual current situation after a job loss, divorce, medical crisis, or other major change. Financial aid offices have a tool for exactly this problem — it's called a special circumstances appeal, or professional judgment — and it's far less well known than it should be.

What situations typically qualify

  • Job loss or reduced income — layoff, reduced hours, or a career change that lowered household income
  • Divorce or separation — since FAFSA income data may still reflect a combined household that no longer exists
  • Death in the family — particularly a parent or spouse, which changes both household size and income
  • Unusually high unreimbursed medical or dental expenses — costs not covered by insurance that meaningfully strain the family budget
  • A one-time payment — a retirement account withdrawal, legal settlement, or similar income spike that inflated the prior year's reported income without reflecting ongoing reality

Federal law lists examples a school may consider — changes in employment, income or assets; medical, dental or nursing home costs not covered by insurance; child or dependent care; and severe disability in the household — but the list isn't exhaustive and each school decides case by case. A school may deny an individual request, but it may not keep a policy of denying all special circumstance requests.

How the process generally works

  1. Complete your FAFSA first, using the required tax year information as instructed — don't try to preemptively adjust the numbers yourself.
  2. Contact each school's financial aid office directly and ask for their special circumstances or professional judgment form — the name varies by school.
  3. Gather supporting documentation specific to your situation (see below) and submit it with a clear written explanation.
  4. Wait for the financial aid office's review — this is handled case by case, the school must document why it approved or denied the request, and the financial aid administrator's decision is final — it cannot be appealed to the Department of Education.

An approved appeal doesn't guarantee more aid. It means your eligibility gets recalculated with updated information — the result could be more aid, the same amount, or occasionally no change, particularly if your Student Aid Index was already at or near zero. It's still worth doing if your circumstances have genuinely changed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What situations typically qualify for a FAFSA special circumstances appeal? Common qualifying situations include job loss, divorce, a death in the family, high unreimbursed medical expenses, and one-time payments that inflated prior-year income. Each school has discretion over what it accepts.

Does a special circumstances appeal guarantee more aid? No. An approved appeal means recalculation with updated information, which could result in more aid, the same amount, or occasionally no change.

When should I submit a special circumstances appeal? Submit your regular FAFSA first, then contact each school's financial aid office directly to request their specific form.

What documentation do I need? Typical documentation includes a termination letter, divorce decree, death certificate, or medical bills, depending on your situation, along with a written explanation. Confirm the specific list with each school.

I'm a first-generation student and my parents can't help me navigate this — where do I start? You're not expected to figure this out alone. Your financial aid office holds workshops and one-on-one appointments specifically for this, and many schools have staff whose job is helping first-generation students through exactly this paperwork. It's common for parents to be just as unfamiliar with the terminology as you are — that's normal, and it doesn't affect your eligibility for a special circumstances appeal if your situation otherwise qualifies.
