Is Your Grad Program a "Professional Degree" Under OBBBA?

7 min read · Updated October 2026

Since the OBBBA federal loan limits took effect on July 1, 2026, one classification question has caused more confusion — and more litigation — than any other: is your specific graduate program a "professional degree" (higher $50,000/year, $200,000 lifetime cap) or a regular "graduate" program (lower $20,500/year, $100,000 lifetime cap)? The Department of Education's answer to that question has already changed twice since late June 2026, and the underlying court case is still active. This page tracks the state of play as of publication and links you to where to check for the latest.

Why this fight is happening

The Department's rule recognized only 11 fields as "professional," the traditionally licensed ones such as medicine, dentistry, law, pharmacy and veterinary medicine, and added extra requirements (a doctoral-level degree, six years of postsecondary study, specific CIP codes). Nurse practitioner, physician assistant and other health-profession groups sued. On June 24, 2026, Judge Beryl Howell of the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia stayed those added requirements, without blocking the statutory loan caps. The Department responded on June 29 with a temporary list of 29 six-digit CIP codes and removed 25 others that had been covered at the four-digit level, then issued a July 10 update clarifying that the registered nursing and nursing practice codes cover any program awarding the same credential and adding the Ph.D. to clinical psychology.

What's currently treated as "professional" (as of this update)

Under the June 29 list (updated July 10), the codes treated as professional are: veterinary medicine, law, divinity/ministry (M.Div.) and rabbinical studies; eight clinical and counseling psychology fields; and 17 health fields — chiropractic, audiology, speech-language pathology, dentistry, anesthesiologist assistant, physician assistant, athletic training, medicine (MD and DO), podiatry, optometry, pharmacy (Pharm.D.), occupational therapy, physical therapy, registered nursing (MSN), nurse anesthesia (DNAP) and nursing practice (DNP). Removed at the six-digit level were theology/theological studies, pre-theology, pharmaceutical sciences and other pharmacy specialties, "medicine, other," and psychology fields such as industrial-organizational, educational and applied behavior analysis.

Notably still excluded, and actively contested: social work (MSW), education, public health and MBA programs are not on the list, and nursing Ph.D. programs remain excluded; on August 3, 2026 the plaintiff coalition amended its complaint to challenge those nursing exclusions. Separate challenges are pending in federal courts in Maryland (brought by 25 states and D.C.) and Massachusetts. If you're in an excluded field, budget under the lower graduate caps unless your school tells you otherwise.

This list will likely keep moving

Because the underlying lawsuit hasn't been resolved, this is genuinely a moving target rather than a settled rule. The Department itself has described its more recent changes as "temporary," pending the outcome of litigation — meaning the list could expand further, or in theory contract again, depending on how the case proceeds. Don't treat any single article, including this one, as the final word for your specific program.

How to check your own program

  • Ask your financial aid office directly. They can confirm your program's exact CIP code and current classification — this is the single most reliable source for your situation.
  • Check Federal Student Aid's official announcements at fsapartners.ed.gov, where the Department publishes each electronic announcement as revisions happen.
  • Don't assume based on prestige or difficulty. Classification runs on specific CIP codes and degree credentials, not on how selective or demanding a program feels.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does it matter whether my program counts as "professional"? Professional students can borrow up to $50,000 per year and $200,000 total in federal Direct Unsubsidized Loans. Graduate students who aren't on the professional list are capped at $20,500 per year and $100,000 total — less than half. For programs where tuition regularly exceeds $200,000 total, that classification alone can be the difference between fully funding your program federally and needing tens of thousands in private loans.

Why has the list changed more than once? The Department's original rule recognized only 11 programs as "professional." Several healthcare associations sued, arguing that nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy, and similar programs were unfairly excluded. A federal court stayed the narrower definition on June 24, 2026; the Department then published a temporary list of 29 program codes on June 29 and clarified it on July 10, 2026. The litigation is still ongoing.

What if my field isn't mentioned anywhere on this page? Treat it as a graduate-level program under the $20,500/$100,000 caps unless your school tells you otherwise. Fields like social work and education were not included in the expanded list as of this writing, despite active advocacy for their addition.

Where can I check the official, current list myself? Your school's financial aid office has the most current, authoritative answer for your specific program. You can also check Federal Student Aid's electronic announcements directly at fsapartners.ed.gov.
