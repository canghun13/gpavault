Is Your Grad Program a "Professional Degree" Under OBBBA?

7 min read · Updated July 2026
Advertisement · Google AdSense

Since the OBBBA federal loan limits took effect on July 1, 2026, one classification question has caused more confusion — and more litigation — than any other: is your specific graduate program a "professional degree" (higher $50,000/year, $200,000 lifetime cap) or a regular "graduate" program (lower $20,500/year, $100,000 lifetime cap)? The Department of Education's answer to that question has already changed twice in the last month, and the underlying court case is still active. This page tracks the state of play as of publication and links you to where to check for the latest.

Why this fight is happening

The Department's original rule, published in a May 2026 final rule, recognized only 11 programs as "professional" — the traditionally licensed fields like medicine, dentistry, law, pharmacy, and veterinary medicine. Several healthcare trade associations, representing nurse practitioners, physician assistants, and similar advanced-practice clinicians, sued almost immediately, arguing the narrow definition unfairly excluded programs that are just as rigorous and just as tied to a specific professional license. A federal judge granted a preliminary stay in late June 2026, and the Department responded by publishing an expanded list of 29 programs on June 29 — then issued a further correction on July 10 to fix a coding problem affecting several nursing degree types and to add back a clinical psychology doctoral track that had been left off by mistake.

What's currently treated as "professional" (as of this update)

Based on the Department's most recent guidance, the professional-degree list now includes the traditional core group — medicine (MD/DO), dentistry, law, pharmacy, veterinary medicine, optometry, and similar licensed clinical and legal fields — plus, following the court-ordered expansion, a number of advanced healthcare programs including nursing practice and nursing science degrees (MSN and DNP tracks), physical therapy, occupational therapy, audiology, physician assistant studies, and at least one clinical psychology doctoral pathway. Two fields that had briefly appeared on an earlier draft — theology/theological studies and pharmaceutical studies — were removed in the most recent revision.

Notably still excluded, and actively contested: master's in social work (MSW) and education-focused graduate degrees were not part of the expanded list as of this writing, even though advocacy groups have publicly pushed back on the omission. If you're in one of these fields, budget under the lower graduate caps unless your school tells you otherwise.

This list will likely keep moving

Because the underlying lawsuit hasn't been resolved, this is genuinely a moving target rather than a settled rule. The Department itself has described its more recent changes as "temporary," pending the outcome of litigation — meaning the list could expand further, or in theory contract again, depending on how the case proceeds. Don't treat any single article, including this one, as the final word for your specific program.

How to check your own program

  • Ask your financial aid office directly. They can confirm your program's exact CIP code and current classification — this is the single most reliable source for your situation.
  • Check Federal Student Aid's official announcements at fsapartners.ed.gov, where the Department publishes each electronic announcement as revisions happen.
  • Don't assume based on prestige or difficulty. Classification runs on specific CIP codes and degree credentials, not on how selective or demanding a program feels.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does it matter whether my program counts as "professional"? Professional students can borrow up to $50,000 per year and $200,000 total in federal Direct Unsubsidized Loans. Graduate students who aren't on the professional list are capped at $20,500 per year and $100,000 total — less than half. For programs where tuition regularly exceeds $200,000 total, that classification alone can be the difference between fully funding your program federally and needing tens of thousands in private loans.

Why has the list changed more than once? The Department's original rule recognized only 11 programs as "professional." Several healthcare associations sued, arguing that nursing, physical therapy, occupational therapy, and similar programs were unfairly excluded. A federal court granted a preliminary stay in June 2026, forcing an expansion to 29 programs, followed by a correction on July 10, 2026. The litigation is still ongoing.

What if my field isn't mentioned anywhere on this page? Treat it as a graduate-level program under the $20,500/$100,000 caps unless your school tells you otherwise. Fields like social work and education were not included in the expanded list as of this writing, despite active advocacy for their addition.

Where can I check the official, current list myself? Your school's financial aid office has the most current, authoritative answer for your specific program. You can also check Federal Student Aid's electronic announcements directly at fsapartners.ed.gov.
